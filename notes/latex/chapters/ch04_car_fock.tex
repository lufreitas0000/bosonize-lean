\chapter{CAR Representation and Fock Space}
\label{ch:car_fock}

\section{Introduction and Abstract Setting}

In quantum field theory, the fundamental kinematics of fermions is governed by the Canonical Anticommutation Relations (CAR). Before assigning spatial or momentum coordinates to fermionic modes, the algebraic theory of fermions must be established over a generic, abstract set of single-particle states.

Let $\iota$ be a non-empty, finite index set equipped with a linear order:
\begin{equation}
\iota = \{1, 2, \dots, d\}, \quad d \coloneqq \#\iota \ge 1.
\end{equation}
The choice of a total linear order $\le$ on $\iota$ is physically unobservable but mathematically essential: it fixes the sign conventions acquired during the permutation of fermionic creation operators, eliminating sign ambiguities.

Typical physical realizations of $\iota$ include:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The discrete centered Brillouin zone $\iota = \Lambda^* = \{-h+1, \dots, h\}$, linearly ordered by the standard integer order $\le$ (Chapters~\ref{ch:lattice_band} and~\ref{ch:fermions}).
    \item Multi-species or spinful chiral fermions $\iota = \{R, L\} \times \{\uparrow, \downarrow\} \times \Lambda^*$, endowed with the lexicographic product order.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The Two-Tiered CAR Hierarchy: Algebraic vs.\ Hilbert Setting}

An algebra is a vector space equipped with a compatible multiplication; an operator algebra is a collection of linear maps closed under addition, scalar multiplication, and composition. To build the CAR algebra abstractly, start with all finite words in symbols $c_i,c_i^\dagger$, including the empty word $1$. Linear combinations of these words form the free associative algebra: before imposing relations, distinct words are independent formal expressions. The CAR relations generate a two-sided ideal, consisting of sums of those relations multiplied on the left and right by arbitrary words. Quotienting by this ideal identifies expressions that the CAR require to be equal. The resulting abstract algebra records only the relations, not a choice of matrices or Hilbert space.

An algebra representation is a multiplication-preserving linear map from that abstract quotient into $\operatorname{End}(V)$, the space of linear maps $V\to V$ under composition. Its image is the span of the represented words. This distinction matters: an abstract relation is universal, while a representation can have extra identities if its image has a kernel. In the occupation representation below, $V$ is the fermionic Fock space, naturally identified with the exterior algebra $\Lambda^*(\mathbb{C}^d)$. A basis wedge $e_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_r}$ records occupied modes. Permuting its factors acts by the sign of the corresponding permutation in $S_r$; antisymmetry of the exterior product is precisely the source of the fermionic exchange signs.

To separate pure algebraic identities from metric and inner-product properties, we distinguish between two tiers of CAR representations.

\begin{definition}[Algebraic CAR Representation — Tier 1]
Let $V$ be a complex vector space. An \emph{Algebraic CAR representation} over $\iota$ on $V$ consists of two families of linear operators $c, c^\dagger : \iota \to \mathrm{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)$ satisfying, for all $i, j \in \iota$:
\begin{align}
\{ c_i, c_j \} &\coloneqq c_i c_j + c_j c_i = 0, \label{eq:car_ann_ann} \\
\{ c_i^\dagger, c_j^\dagger \} &\coloneqq c_i^\dagger c_j^\dagger + c_j^\dagger c_i^\dagger = 0, \label{eq:car_cre_cre} \\
\{ c_i, c_j^\dagger \} &\coloneqq c_i c_j^\dagger + c_j^\dagger c_i = \delta_{i, j} I_V. \label{eq:car_ann_cre}
\end{align}
\end{definition}

Without assuming any inner product, adjoint operation, or topological structure on $V$, the algebraic relations alone imply fundamental commutation theorems.

\begin{theorem}[Purely Algebraic CAR Identities]
\label{thm:algebraic_car}
Let $(c, c^\dagger)$ be an Algebraic CAR representation on $V$.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Bilinear Commutator Identity:} For any modes $a, b, c, d \in \iota$:
    \begin{equation}
    \left[ c_a^\dagger c_b, c_c^\dagger c_d \right] = \delta_{b, c}\, c_a^\dagger c_d - \delta_{a, d}\, c_c^\dagger c_b.
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Number Operator Commutators:} For $n_i \coloneqq c_i^\dagger c_i$ and any $j \in \iota$:
    \begin{align}
    [n_i, c_j^\dagger] &= \delta_{i, j} c_j^\dagger, \\
    [n_i, c_j] &= -\delta_{i, j} c_j.
    \end{align}
    \item \textbf{Idempotence and Number Commutativity:}
    \begin{equation}
    n_i^2 = n_i, \qquad [n_i, n_j] = 0 \quad \text{for all } i, j \in \iota.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We verify each algebraic identity:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Expanding the commutator and applying $c_b c_c^\dagger=\delta_{b,c}I-c_c^\dagger c_b$ and $c_d c_a^\dagger=\delta_{a,d}I-c_a^\dagger c_d$ gives
    \begin{equation}
    [c_a^\dagger c_b,c_c^\dagger c_d]
    =\delta_{b,c}c_a^\dagger c_d-\delta_{a,d}c_c^\dagger c_b
    -c_a^\dagger c_c^\dagger c_b c_d+c_c^\dagger c_a^\dagger c_d c_b.
    \end{equation}
    The CAR relations $\{c_a^\dagger,c_c^\dagger\}=0$ and $\{c_b,c_d\}=0$ turn the last quartic term into $+c_a^\dagger c_c^\dagger c_b c_d$, which cancels the preceding $-c_a^\dagger c_c^\dagger c_b c_d$. This leaves the claimed bilinear identity.
    \item Setting $a = b = i$ and $c = j$ in the bilinear identity gives $[n_i, c_j^\dagger c_d] = \delta_{i, j} c_i^\dagger c_d - \delta_{i, d} c_j^\dagger c_i$. Contracting with a test state or using $\{c_i, c_j^\dagger\} = \delta_{i,j}$:
    \begin{equation}
    n_i c_j^\dagger = c_i^\dagger c_i c_j^\dagger = c_i^\dagger (\delta_{i, j} I - c_j^\dagger c_i) = \delta_{i, j} c_j^\dagger - c_i^\dagger c_j^\dagger c_i = \delta_{i, j} c_j^\dagger + c_j^\dagger c_i^\dagger c_i = \delta_{i, j} c_j^\dagger + c_j^\dagger n_i,
    \end{equation}
    which gives $[n_i, c_j^\dagger] = \delta_{i, j} c_j^\dagger$. The annihilation commutator follows analogously.
    
    \item For idempotence: $n_i^2 = c_i^\dagger c_i c_i^\dagger c_i = c_i^\dagger (I - c_i^\dagger c_i) c_i = c_i^\dagger c_i - (c_i^\dagger)^2 c_i^2$. Since $(c_i^\dagger)^2 = 0$, $n_i^2 = n_i$. Finally, $[n_i, n_j] = [c_i^\dagger c_i, c_j^\dagger c_j] = \delta_{i, j} c_i^\dagger c_j - \delta_{i, j} c_j^\dagger c_i = 0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Hilbert Space CAR Representation — Tier 2]
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional complex Hilbert space equipped with positive-definite inner product $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$. A \emph{Hilbert CAR representation} over $\iota$ on $V$ is an Algebraic CAR representation $(c, c^\dagger)$ that additionally satisfies \emph{Hilbert Adjoint Compatibility}:
\begin{equation}
c_i^\dagger = (c_i)^\dagger \quad \text{for all } i \in \iota,
\end{equation}
where $(A)^\dagger$ denotes the unique Hilbert space adjoint satisfying $\langle u, A v \rangle = \langle A^\dagger u, v \rangle$.
\end{definition}

The anticommutator equations above have two related roles. First, they define
the abstract CAR $*$-algebra: finite words in the generators are identified
whenever the relations require them to be equal, and the involution exchanges
$c_i$ with $c_i^\dagger$. Second, a representation of this algebra on a
Hilbert space assigns concrete operators to those generators. In that
representation the displayed CAR equations still hold, while the additional
condition $c_i^\dagger=(c_i)^\dagger_{\!H}$ says that the algebraic
involution is realized by the Hilbert-space adjoint. Thus the algebraic
relations define the abstract object and are also obeyed by its physical
Fock representation; adjoint compatibility is the extra Hilbert-space
requirement.

\paragraph{Bra-Ket Matrix Elements and Operator Notation.}
In Dirac bra-ket notation, for any states $|u\rangle, |v\rangle \in V$ and linear operator $A \in \mathrm{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)$, the matrix element is written using vertical bars:
\begin{equation}
\langle u \mid A \mid v \rangle \coloneqq \langle u \mid A v \rangle \equiv \langle A^\dagger u \mid v \rangle.
\end{equation}
This notation is completely rigorous: the placement of the operator between the bra and ket reflects the self-adjointness of the inner product pairing, meaning that acting forward on the ket with $A$ is identical to acting backward on the bra with $A^\dagger$.

\begin{remark}[Scope of the finite statements]
Each result below is stated on its specified finite carrier and under its displayed hypotheses. The algebraic and combinatorial identities are exact on those carriers; they do not require an analytic limit or assert a stronger continuum identity.
\end{remark}

\section{The Concrete Occupation Fock Space}

We now construct the canonical Fock space representation of the CAR algebra.

\begin{definition}[Occupation Basis and Fock Space Carrier]
Let $\iota$ be a finite set. The fermionic Fock space $\mathcal{F}(\iota)$ is the complex Euclidean space indexed by the power set $\mathcal{P}(\iota)$ of all subsets of $\iota$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}(\iota) \coloneqq \ell^2(\mathcal{P}(\iota)) \equiv \bigoplus_{S \subseteq \iota} \mathbb{C} |S\rangle.
\end{equation}
For each subset $S \subseteq \iota$, the basis vector $|S\rangle$ is the indicator state representing the configuration where exactly the single-particle modes in $S$ are occupied.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Dimension and Inner Product]
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The complex dimension of the Fock space is:
    \begin{equation}
    \dim_{\mathbb{C}} \mathcal{F}(\iota) = 2^{\#\iota} = 2^d.
    \end{equation}
    \item Basis states form an orthonormal basis under the standard Euclidean inner product:
    \begin{equation}
    \langle S \mid T \rangle = \delta_{S, T} \quad \text{for all } S, T \subseteq \iota.
    \end{equation}
    \item The state $|\emptyset\rangle$ is the unique normalized fermionic vacuum state.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\section{Exact Fermionic Sign Mechanics}

To define creation and annihilation operators on $\mathcal{F}(\iota)$ that satisfy anticommutation, we must account for the permutation phase acquired when inserting or removing a fermion.

\begin{definition}[Preceding Count and Fermionic Sign]
Let $\iota$ be linearly ordered by $\le$. For any mode $i \in \iota$ and any occupation subset $S \subseteq \iota$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The \emph{preceding count} $\sigma(i, S) \in \mathbb{N}$ is the number of occupied modes in $S$ strictly preceding $i$:
    \begin{equation}
    \sigma(i, S) \coloneqq \#\{j \in S \mid j < i\}.
    \end{equation}
    \item The \emph{fermionic sign} $\epsilon(i, S)$ is the sign factor:
    \begin{equation}
    \epsilon(i, S) \coloneqq (-1)^{\sigma(i, S)} \in \{+1, -1\}.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

To maintain strict mathematical precision, the domain of the count $\sigma(i, S)$ is defined in $\mathbb{N}$, and arithmetic evaluations are carried out additively via disjoint unions.

\begin{lemma}[Additive Atomic Counts]
\label{lem:atomic_counts}
Let $i, j \in \iota$ and $S \subseteq \iota$.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item If $j \notin S$, then:
    \begin{equation}
    \sigma(i, S \cup \{j\}) = \sigma(i, S) + \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } j < i, \\ 0, & \text{if } j \ge i. \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    \item If $j \in S$, then:
    \begin{equation}
    \sigma(i, S) = \sigma(i, S \setminus \{j\}) + \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } j < i, \\ 0, & \text{if } j \ge i. \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    \item Self-invariance: $\sigma(i, S \cup \{i\}) = \sigma(i, S)$ and $\sigma(i, S \setminus \{i\}) = \sigma(i, S)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The preceding set $\{k \in S \cup \{j\} \mid k < i\}$ is the disjoint union of $\{k \in S \mid k < i\}$ and $\{j\}$ if $j < i$, or simply $\{k \in S \mid k < i\}$ if $j \ge i$. Taking set cardinalities yields the additive formula. Part (2) is the inverse statement, and part (3) follows because $i < i$ is false.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Pedagogical Context and Physical Interpretation of Fermionic Signs.}
Why are fermionic signs $\epsilon(i, S)$ necessary, and how do they ensure anticommutation?
In quantum mechanics, multi-particle wavefunctions for fermions must be completely antisymmetric under particle exchange: $\Psi(x_1, x_2) = - \Psi(x_2, x_1)$. When formulating this on an occupation basis $|S\rangle$, each state is conceptually an ordered product of creation operators acting on the vacuum:
\begin{equation}
|S\rangle = c_{i_k}^\dagger \cdots c_{i_2}^\dagger c_{i_1}^\dagger |\emptyset\rangle \quad \text{with } i_1 < i_2 < \dots < i_k.
\end{equation}
When an additional fermion is created at mode $j \notin S$, the operator $c_j^\dagger$ must be commuted past all existing particles with modes $p < j$ to reach its canonical position in the ordered product. Since adjacent fermionic operators anticommute ($\{c_p^\dagger, c_j^\dagger\} = 0$), each permutation past an occupied mode contributes a factor of $(-1)$. Therefore, the insertion of particle $j$ incurs the phase $(-1)^{\sigma(j, S)} = \epsilon(j, S)$.

When two operations are performed sequentially, say creating mode $j$ and then creating mode $i$ (with $j < i$), two distinct orderings are possible:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Creating $j$ then $i$:} When $j$ is created first, it enters the state with phase $\epsilon(j, S)$. When $i$ is created next, mode $j$ is already present in the configuration. Because $j < i$, mode $j$ contributes $+1$ to the count of particles preceding $i$, so the second phase is $(-1)^{\sigma(i, S) + 1} = -\epsilon(i, S)$.
    \item \textbf{Creating $i$ then $j$:} When $i$ is created first, it enters with phase $\epsilon(i, S)$. When $j$ is created next, although $i$ is now in the configuration, $i > j$ does \emph{not} precede $j$! Thus the count of particles preceding $j$ is unaffected: $\sigma(j, S \cup \{i\}) = \sigma(j, S)$, so the phase remains $\epsilon(j, S)$.
\end{enumerate}
Comparing the two composite phases reveals a relative factor of $-1$. This fundamental sign discrepancy is the exact combinatorial origin of $\{c_i^\dagger, c_j^\dagger\} = 0$.

\begin{theorem}[Fermionic Sign Exchange Identities]
\label{thm:sign_exchange}
For any distinct modes $i \neq j$ in $\iota$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Creation Pair:} If $i, j \notin S$:
    \begin{equation}
    \epsilon(j, S)\, \epsilon(i, S \cup \{j\}) = - \epsilon(i, S)\, \epsilon(j, S \cup \{i\}).
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Annihilation Pair:} If $i, j \in S$:
    \begin{equation}
    \epsilon(j, S)\, \epsilon(i, S \setminus \{j\}) = - \epsilon(i, S)\, \epsilon(j, S \setminus \{i\}).
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Mixed Pair:} If $i \notin S$ and $j \in S$:
    \begin{equation}
    \epsilon(j, S)\, \epsilon(i, S \setminus \{j\}) = - \epsilon(i, S)\, \epsilon(j, S \cup \{i\}).
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose first that $j<i$. Adding $j$ shifts the preceding count for $i$ by one, while adding $i$ leaves the count for $j$ unchanged:
\begin{equation}
\sigma(i,S\cup\{j\})=\sigma(i,S)+1,\qquad
\sigma(j,S\cup\{i\})=\sigma(j,S).
\end{equation}
Thus $\epsilon(i,S\cup\{j\})=-\epsilon(i,S)$ and $\epsilon(j,S\cup\{i\})=\epsilon(j,S)$, giving the relative factor $-1$ in part (1). If $i<j$, exchange their roles. For removal and mixed pairs, the corresponding count changes follow from Lemma~\ref{lem:atomic_counts}(2) in the same way, yielding parts (2) and (3).
\end{proof}

\section{Creation and Annihilation Operators on Fock Space}

\begin{definition}[Fermionic Operators on Basis States]
For each mode $i \in \iota$, the annihilation operator $c_i$ and creation operator $c_i^\dagger$ are defined on basis states $|S\rangle$ by:
\begin{align}
c_i |S\rangle &\coloneqq \begin{cases}
\epsilon(i, S) |S \setminus \{i\}\rangle, & \text{if } i \in S, \\
0, & \text{if } i \notin S,
\end{cases} \label{eq:fock_c} \\
c_i^\dagger |S\rangle &\coloneqq \begin{cases}
\epsilon(i, S) |S \cup \{i\}\rangle, & \text{if } i \notin S, \\
0, & \text{if } i \in S,
\end{cases} \label{eq:fock_cdag}
\end{align}
and extended linearly to all states in $\mathcal{F}(\iota)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Verification of the Concrete CAR Representation]
\label{thm:fock_car_verification}
The operators $\{c_i, c_i^\dagger \mid i \in \iota\}$ satisfy:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Adjoint Compatibility:} $c_i^\dagger = (c_i)^\dagger$ under the Euclidean inner product.
    \item \textbf{Anticommutation Relations:}
    \begin{equation}
    \{c_i, c_j\} = 0, \qquad \{c_i^\dagger, c_j^\dagger\} = 0, \qquad \{c_i, c_j^\dagger\} = \delta_{i, j} I.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
In particular, $\mathcal{F}(\iota)$ is a concrete Hilbert CAR representation.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We verify both properties on arbitrary basis states $|S\rangle, |T\rangle$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item For the adjoint relation, both matrix elements $\langle S\mid c_i\mid T\rangle$ and $\langle c_i^\dagger S\mid T\rangle$ vanish unless $i\in T$ and $S=T\setminus\{i\}$. In that case their coefficients are respectively $\epsilon(i,T)$ and $\epsilon(i,S)$, which are equal because removing $i$ does not change the number of occupied modes preceding $i$, so $\sigma(i,S)=\sigma(i,T)$. Equality on all basis pairs gives $c_i^\dagger=(c_i)^\dagger$.

    \item For anticommutators:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Same-mode anticommutator:} If $i = j$, $c_i^2 |S\rangle = 0$ because $i$ cannot be removed twice, and $(c_i^\dagger)^2 |S\rangle = 0$ because $i$ cannot be inserted twice. For the mixed product: $c_i c_i^\dagger |S\rangle = |S\rangle$ if $i \notin S$ and $0$ if $i \in S$; while $c_i^\dagger c_i |S\rangle = |S\rangle$ if $i \in S$ and $0$ if $i \notin S$. Summing the two yields $\{c_i, c_i^\dagger\} |S\rangle = |S\rangle = I |S\rangle$.
        \item \textbf{Distinct-mode anticommutator:} If $i \neq j$, applying $c_i^\dagger c_j^\dagger |S\rangle$ yields $\epsilon(j, S) \epsilon(i, S \cup \{j\}) |S \cup \{i, j\}\rangle$ if $i, j \notin S$. Applying $c_j^\dagger c_i^\dagger |S\rangle$ yields $\epsilon(i, S) \epsilon(j, S \cup \{i\}) |S \cup \{i, j\}\rangle$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:sign_exchange}(1), these two coefficients are exact negatives of each other, so their sum vanishes: $\{c_i^\dagger, c_j^\dagger\} |S\rangle = 0$. Annihilation pairs and mixed pairs vanish identically by parts (2) and (3) of Theorem~\ref{thm:sign_exchange}.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

\section{The Global Fermion Parity Operator}

\begin{definition}[Global Fermion Parity]
Let the single-particle index set $\iota = \{i_1 < i_2 < \dots < i_d\}$ be ordered. The \emph{Global Fermion Parity Operator} $\Gamma \in \mathrm{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{F}(\iota))$ is defined by:
\begin{equation}
\Gamma \coloneqq \prod_{k=1}^d (I - 2 n_{i_k}).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Parity Properties]
\label{thm:parity_properties}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item On any occupation basis state $|S\rangle$:
    \begin{equation}
    \Gamma |S\rangle = (-1)^{\#S} |S\rangle.
    \end{equation}
    \item $\Gamma$ is self-adjoint and unitary: $\Gamma^\dagger = \Gamma$ and $\Gamma^2 = I$.
    \item $\Gamma$ anticommutes with all fermionic generators:
    \begin{equation}
    \Gamma c_i = - c_i \Gamma, \qquad \Gamma c_i^\dagger = - c_i^\dagger \Gamma \quad \text{for all } i \in \iota.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
On basis state $|S\rangle$, each factor $(I - 2 n_k)$ evaluates to $-1$ if $k \in S$, and $+1$ if $k \notin S$. Taking the product over all $d$ modes yields $\prod_{k \in S} (-1) \prod_{k \notin S} (+1) = (-1)^{\#S}$.
Since $(-1)^{\#S} \in \{+1, -1\}$, $\Gamma^2 |S\rangle = ((-1)^{\#S})^2 |S\rangle = |S\rangle$, so $\Gamma^2 = I$. Self-adjointness follows because eigenvalues are real.
For anticommutation: $c_i$ changes the particle number by $-1$, so $\#(S \setminus \{i\}) = \#S - 1$.
Therefore $\Gamma c_i |S\rangle = (-1)^{\#S - 1} c_i |S\rangle = - c_i (-1)^{\#S} |S\rangle = - c_i \Gamma |S\rangle$.
Taking adjoints yields $\Gamma c_i^\dagger = - c_i^\dagger \Gamma$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Clifford Algebra and the Fock Spin Representation}

Write the ordered modes as $i_1<\cdots<i_d$ and let $W=\mathbb C^d$ with its standard inner product and ordered orthonormal basis $e_1,\ldots,e_d$. Identify $\mathcal F(\iota)$ with the exterior algebra $\Lambda^*W$ by mapping $|S\rangle$ to the wedge of those $e_j$ for which $i_j\in S$, in increasing order. For each $j$, exterior multiplication and contraction are linear maps on this same finite-dimensional space:
\begin{equation}
C_j(\omega)=e_j\wedge\omega,\qquad A_j(\omega)=\iota_{e_j}\omega,
\qquad C_j,A_j\in\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb C}(\Lambda^*W).
\end{equation}
The defining contraction identity
\begin{equation}
\iota_{e_j}(e_k\wedge\omega)+e_k\wedge\iota_{e_j}(\omega)=\delta_{jk}\omega
\end{equation}
gives the CAR, with $C_j=c_{i_j}^\dagger$ and $A_j=c_{i_j}$ under the occupation-space identification.

The complex Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}_{2d}(\mathbb C)$ is the quotient of the tensor algebra on a $2d$-dimensional space with orthonormal basis by the relations $v w+w v=2b(v,w)1$, where $b$ is the complex bilinear extension of the Euclidean inner product. Equivalently, its generators $\gamma_1,\ldots,\gamma_{2d}$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
\gamma_a\gamma_b+\gamma_b\gamma_a=2\delta_{ab}1.
\end{equation}
On $\mathcal F(\iota)$ define the Majorana operators
\begin{equation}
\gamma_{2j-1}=c_{i_j}+c_{i_j}^\dagger,\qquad
\gamma_{2j}=-i(c_{i_j}-c_{i_j}^\dagger),\qquad j=1,\ldots,d.
\end{equation}
They are self-adjoint and obey the Clifford relations, so they determine a representation $\rho:\mathrm{Cl}_{2d}(\mathbb C)\to\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb C}(\mathcal F(\iota))$. For each mode,
\begin{equation}
\gamma_{2j-1}\gamma_{2j}=i(I-2n_{i_j}).
\end{equation}
The even factors for distinct modes commute. Consequently their ordered product is the Clifford volume element and is related to parity by
\begin{equation}
\gamma_1\gamma_2\cdots\gamma_{2d}=i^d\Gamma,
\qquad \Gamma=(-i)^d\gamma_1\gamma_2\cdots\gamma_{2d}.
\end{equation}

This representation is irreducible. Since each $c_{i_j}$ and $c_{i_j}^\dagger$ is a linear combination of Majorana generators, the represented Clifford algebra contains the occupation projections
\begin{equation}
P_S=\prod_{i\in S}n_i\prod_{i\notin S}(I-n_i)
\end{equation}
which select the individual basis states. Strings of creation and annihilation operators carry $|S\rangle$ to any chosen $|T\rangle$ with a nonzero sign. After correcting that sign, these operators give every matrix unit $|T\rangle\langle S|$. Thus $\rho$ is onto the full endomorphism algebra. Since $\dim\mathcal F(\iota)=2^d$, its endomorphism algebra has dimension $2^{2d}$; the Clifford relations span $\mathrm{Cl}_{2d}(\mathbb C)$ by at most $2^{2d}$ ordered monomials. Surjectivity forces equality of dimensions, so $\rho$ is an isomorphism. The full matrix algebra acts irreducibly, and $\Gamma$ is the grading operator, acting by $(-1)^r$ on $\Lambda^r W$.
