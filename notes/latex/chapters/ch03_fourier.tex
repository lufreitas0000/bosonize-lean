\chapter{The Finite Fourier Transform}
\label{ch:fourier}

\section{Introduction and Algebraic Setting}

The discrete Fourier transform is the central kinematic bridge of quantum field theory on a lattice. It connects the spatial coordinate representation on the periodic lattice $\Lambda = \mathbb{Z}/L\mathbb{Z}$ to the dual momentum representation on the centered band $\Lambda^* = \{-h+1, \dots, h\}$ (for even $L = 2h$).

In standard physics presentations, the Fourier transform is often introduced on complex functions with exponential characters and the unitary factor $1/\sqrt{L}$. It is useful to separate the algebraic character identities from the Hilbert-space normalization. The former require a field $K$ containing a primitive $L$-th root of unity $\zeta$; two-sided inversion additionally requires $L\cdot1_K\ne0$. The unitary transform is the further specialization to $K=\mathbb C$.

The field condition cannot in general be replaced by an arbitrary commutative ring, even when the root has exact order and $L$ is a unit. For example, let $L=2$ and $R=\mathbb Z/15\mathbb Z$. The element $\zeta=4$ has exact multiplicative order two, since $4\not\equiv1\pmod{15}$ and $4^2\equiv1\pmod{15}$. Moreover, $2\cdot8\equiv1\pmod{15}$, so $L$ is invertible in $R$. Yet the nontrivial character sum is
\begin{equation}
\sum_{x=0}^{1}\zeta^x=1+4=5\not\equiv0\pmod{15}.
\end{equation}
Here $\zeta-1=3$ is a zero divisor, and $(\zeta-1)(1+\zeta)=0$ does not force $1+\zeta=0$. Thus exact order and invertibility of $L$ alone do not guarantee character cancellation over rings with zero divisors.

The geometric-sum argument does work over a field: every nonzero $1-\zeta^m$ is invertible for a nontrivial frequency. For a general commutative ring, it suffices instead to require each such factor to be a unit; inversion further requires $L\cdot1_R$ to be a unit. We use fields for the general algebraic theory below.

\section{Generic Character Algebra over Fields}

\begin{definition}[Primitive Root of Unity]
Let $K$ be a field and $L\ge1$. An element $\zeta\in K$ is a primitive $L$-th root of unity when
\begin{equation}
\zeta^L=1,\qquad \zeta^m\ne1\quad(1\le m<L).
\end{equation}
For $K=\mathbb C$, the canonical choice is $\zeta_{\mathrm{can}}=\exp(2\pi i/L)$.
\end{definition}

The characters are defined in three related forms. The integer pairing makes no quotient choices, the residue character descends to residue classes, and the band character uses the centered momentum representatives of Chapter~\ref{ch:lattice_band}.

\begin{definition}[Three-Tier Character]
For $k,x\in\mathbb Z$, the integer character pairing is
\begin{equation}
\chi_{\mathbb Z}(k,x)\coloneqq\zeta^{kx}.
\end{equation}
For residue classes $k,x\in\mathbb Z/L\mathbb Z$, with canonical representatives $k_{\mathrm{val}},x_{\mathrm{val}}\in\{0,\ldots,L-1\}$, define
\begin{equation}
\chi_{\mathrm{res}}(k,x)\coloneqq\chi_{\mathbb Z}(k_{\mathrm{val}},x_{\mathrm{val}}).
\end{equation}
For a band mode $k\in\Lambda^*$ and a site $x\in\Lambda$, define
\begin{equation}
\chi(k,x)\coloneqq\chi_{\mathrm{band}}(k,x)\coloneqq\chi_{\mathbb Z}(k,x_{\mathrm{val}})
=\chi_{\mathrm{res}}(\pi(k),x),
\end{equation}
where $\pi:\Lambda^*\to\mathbb Z/L\mathbb Z$ is the canonical projection. We use $\chi$ for the band character henceforth.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Representative Independence]
\label{lem:rep_independence}
For $k\in\mathbb Z$ and any representative $\widetilde{x}=x_{\mathrm{val}}+mL$ of $x\in\Lambda$,
\begin{equation}
\zeta^{k\widetilde{x}}=\zeta^{k(x_{\mathrm{val}}+mL)}=\zeta^{kx_{\mathrm{val}}}(\zeta^{kL})^m
=\zeta^{kx_{\mathrm{val}}}(\zeta^L)^{km}=\zeta^{kx_{\mathrm{val}}}=\chi(k,x).
\end{equation}
The integer character is also independent of both residue representatives: for $s,t\in\mathbb Z$,
\begin{equation}
\chi_{\mathbb Z}(k+sL,x+tL)=\chi_{\mathbb Z}(k,x),
\end{equation}
since $(k+sL)(x+tL)-kx$ is an integer multiple of $L$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The first equality follows from $\zeta^L=1$ and $\widetilde{x}=x_{\mathrm{val}}+mL$. For the simultaneous shift, the exponent changes by $L(kt+sx+stL)$, so the character is unchanged.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Character Homomorphism Laws]
\label{thm:character_homomorphisms}
For residue classes $k,p,x,y\in\mathbb Z/L\mathbb Z$ and band modes $u,v\in\Lambda^*$ with sites $x,y\in\Lambda$,
\begin{align}
\chi_{\mathrm{res}}(k,x+y)&=\chi_{\mathrm{res}}(k,x)\chi_{\mathrm{res}}(k,y),\\
\chi_{\mathrm{res}}(k+p,x)&=\chi_{\mathrm{res}}(k,x)\chi_{\mathrm{res}}(p,x),\\
\chi_{\mathrm{res}}(-k,x)&=\chi_{\mathrm{res}}(k,x)^{-1}=\chi_{\mathrm{res}}(k,-x),\\
\chi(u\oplus v,x)&=\chi(u,x)\chi(v,x).
\end{align}
When $K=\mathbb C$ and $|\zeta|=1$, $\overline{\chi(k,x)}=\chi(-k,x)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The first three identities follow from exponent addition and representative independence. For band addition, Theorem~\ref{thm:wrap_lemma} gives $k\oplus p=k+p-wL$ for some integer $w$, so
\begin{equation}
\chi(k\oplus p,x)=\zeta^{(k+p-wL)x_{\mathrm{val}}}
=\zeta^{kx_{\mathrm{val}}}\zeta^{px_{\mathrm{val}}}(\zeta^L)^{-wx_{\mathrm{val}}}
=\chi(k,x)\chi(p,x).
\end{equation}
For $K=\mathbb C$ and $|\zeta|=1$, conjugation sends $\zeta$ to $\zeta^{-1}$, giving the stated conjugation law.
\end{proof}

\section{The Unscaled Analysis and Synthesis Transforms}

Because the spatial lattice $\Lambda$ and the momentum band $\Lambda^*$ are distinct sets, the Fourier transform consists of two directional linear maps between distinct function modules:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Spatial function space: $K^\Lambda \equiv (\Lambda \to K)$.
    \item Momentum function space: $K^{\Lambda^*} \equiv (\Lambda^* \to K)$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{definition}[Analysis and Synthesis Transforms]
We define the $K$-linear maps $S\in\operatorname{Hom}_K(K^\Lambda,K^{\Lambda^*})$ and $T\in\operatorname{Hom}_K(K^{\Lambda^*},K^\Lambda)$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Analysis Transform $S : K^\Lambda \to K^{\Lambda^*}$:}
    \begin{equation}
    (S f)(k) \coloneqq \sum_{x \in \Lambda} f(x)\, \chi(-k, x), \quad k \in \Lambda^*.
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Synthesis Transform $T : K^{\Lambda^*} \to K^\Lambda$:}
    \begin{equation}
    (T g)(x) \coloneqq \sum_{k \in \Lambda^*} g(k)\, \chi(k, x), \quad x \in \Lambda.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Note that $\chi(-k, x) = \zeta^{-k x} = (\zeta^{kx})^{-1} = \chi(k, x)^{-1}$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:rep_independence}, evaluating at $-k \in \mathbb{Z}$ agrees with evaluating at the transported band negation $\ominus k \in \Lambda^*$, ensuring algebraic consistency even when $-k \notin \Lambda^*$.

\section{Dual Character Orthogonality and Algebraic Inversion}

The foundation of Fourier analysis on finite abelian groups is character orthogonality.

\begin{lemma}[Geometric Sum of Roots of Unity]
\label{lem:geom_sum}
Let $\zeta \in K$ be a primitive $L$-th root of unity. For any integer $m \in \mathbb{Z}$:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{x=0}^{L-1} \zeta^{m x} = \begin{cases}
L \cdot 1_K, & \text{if } m \equiv 0 \pmod L, \\
0, & \text{if } m \not\equiv 0 \pmod L.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $m \equiv 0 \pmod L$, then $\zeta^m = 1$, giving $\sum_{x=0}^{L-1} 1 = L \cdot 1_K$.
If $m \not\equiv 0 \pmod L$, then $\zeta^m \neq 1$. Telescoping yields:
\begin{equation}
(1 - \zeta^m) \sum_{x=0}^{L-1} \zeta^{m x} = 1 - \zeta^{m L} = 1 - 1 = 0.
\end{equation}
Because $K$ is a field and $(1 - \zeta^m) \neq 0$, dividing by $(1 - \zeta^m)$ proves the sum is $0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Dual Character Orthogonality]
\label{thm:fourier_orthogonality}
Let $K$ be a field with a primitive $L$-th root of unity $\zeta$.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Spatial Orthogonality:} For any momenta $k, p \in \Lambda^*$:
    \begin{equation}
    \sum_{x \in \Lambda} \chi(k, x)\, \chi(-p, x) = (L \cdot 1_K)\, \delta_{k, p}.
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Momentum Orthogonality:} For any spatial sites $x, y \in \Lambda$:
    \begin{equation}
    \sum_{k \in \Lambda^*} \chi(k, x)\, \chi(-k, y) = (L \cdot 1_K)\, \delta_{x, y}.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For spatial orthogonality, $\chi(k,x)\chi(-p,x)=\zeta^{(k-p)x}$. The geometric sum is $L\cdot1_K$ when $k-p\equiv0\pmod L$, and zero otherwise. Since $-L<k-p<L$, this congruence forces $k=p$, giving $(L\cdot1_K)\delta_{k,p}$.
Momentum orthogonality follows similarly: $\sum_{k \in \Lambda^*} \zeta^{k (x-y)}$. Translating the sum over the centered band $\Lambda^* = \{-h+1, \dots, h\}$ to $\{0, \dots, L-1\}$ introduces an overall phase factor $\zeta^{(-h+1)(x-y)}$ that is non-zero in $K$, leaving the sum proportional to $(L \cdot 1_K) \delta_{x, y}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Two-Sided Algebraic Inversion]
\label{thm:algebraic_inversion}
Assume $L \cdot 1_K \neq 0$ in $K$. Then the transforms $S$ and $T$ satisfy:
\begin{align}
T \circ S &= (L \cdot 1_K)\, I_{K^\Lambda}, \\
S \circ T &= (L \cdot 1_K)\, I_{K^{\Lambda^*}}.
\end{align}
Consequently, both $S$ and $T$ are vector space isomorphisms.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $f\in K^\Lambda$,
\begin{align}
(T(Sf))(x)&=\sum_{k\in\Lambda^*}\sum_{y\in\Lambda}f(y)\chi(-k,y)\chi(k,x)
=\sum_{y\in\Lambda}f(y)\left[\sum_{k\in\Lambda^*}\chi(k,x)\chi(-k,y)\right]\\
&=(L\cdot1_K)f(x),
\end{align}
by momentum orthogonality. The corresponding expansion of $S(Tg)$ and spatial orthogonality give $(S(Tg))(k)=(L\cdot1_K)g(k)$.
\end{proof}

\section{Unitary Fourier Transform in the Hilbert Setting}

When $K = \mathbb{C}$, the function spaces $K^\Lambda$ and $K^{\Lambda^*}$ are complex Euclidean Hilbert spaces equipped with standard inner products:
\begin{equation}
\langle f_1, f_2 \rangle_{\mathrm{pos}} \coloneqq \sum_{x \in \Lambda} \overline{f_1(x)}\, f_2(x), \qquad \langle g_1, g_2 \rangle_{\mathrm{mom}} \coloneqq \sum_{k \in \Lambda^*} \overline{g_1(k)}\, g_2(k).
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[Unitary Fourier Transform]
Let $a \coloneqq 1/\sqrt{L} \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$. The \emph{unitary discrete Fourier transform} $U : \mathbb{C}^\Lambda \to \mathbb{C}^{\Lambda^*}$ is defined by:
\begin{equation}
U \coloneqq \frac{1}{\sqrt{L}} S, \qquad (U f)(k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{L}} \sum_{x \in \Lambda} f(x)\, \chi(-k, x).
\end{equation}
Its inverse is $U^{-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{L}} T$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Unitary Isometry and Plancherel Identity]
\label{thm:unitary_plancherel}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $U^\dagger = U^{-1}$. That is, $U$ is a unitary operator.
    \item \textbf{Parseval/Plancherel Identity:} For all functions $f_1, f_2 \in \mathbb{C}^\Lambda$:
    \begin{equation}
    \langle U f_1, U f_2 \rangle_{\mathrm{mom}} = \langle f_1, f_2 \rangle_{\mathrm{pos}}.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Using $\overline{\chi(-k,x)}=\chi(k,x)$, for $f\in\mathbb C^\Lambda$ and $g\in\mathbb C^{\Lambda^*}$,
\begin{align}
\langle Sf,g\rangle_{\mathrm{mom}}
&=\sum_{k\in\Lambda^*}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\overline{f(x)}\chi(k,x)g(k)
=\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\overline{f(x)}(Tg)(x)
=\langle f,Tg\rangle_{\mathrm{pos}},\\
U^\dagger&=\left(\frac1{\sqrt L}S\right)^\dagger
=\frac1{\sqrt L}S^\dagger
=\frac1{\sqrt L}T=U^{-1}.
\end{align}
Thus $U$ is unitary, and the stated Plancherel identity follows.
\end{proof}

\section{Diagonalization of Difference Operators and Exact Dispersion}

We now connect the Fourier transform to the discrete difference operators of Chapter~\ref{ch:umbral_calculus}.

\begin{theorem}[Diagonal Action on Characters]
\label{thm:diagonal_dispersion}
Let $K$ be a field with a primitive $L$-th root $\zeta$, and let $k\in\Lambda^*$. On $K^\Lambda$, the shift and difference operators satisfy $E,\Delta,\nabla\in\operatorname{End}_K(K^\Lambda)$. Write $\chi_k\in K^\Lambda$ for the function $\chi_k(x)\coloneqq\chi(k,x)$.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The shift operator $E$ acts diagonally:
    \begin{equation}
    (E \chi_k)(x) = \zeta^k \chi_k(x).
    \end{equation}
    \item The forward and backward differences act diagonally:
    \begin{align}
    (\Delta \chi_k)(x) &= (\zeta^k - 1) \chi_k(x), \\
    (\nabla \chi_k)(x) &= (1 - \zeta^{-k}) \chi_k(x).
    \end{align}
    \item Over any field $K$ containing $\zeta$, the discrete Laplacian acts diagonally with the algebraic eigenvalue $\lambda_k=\zeta^k+\zeta^{-k}-2$:
    \begin{equation}
    (\Delta\nabla \chi_k)(x)=\lambda_k\chi_k(x),\qquad \lambda_k=\zeta^k+\zeta^{-k}-2.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Evaluating the definitions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $(E \chi_k)(x) = \chi_k(x+1) = \zeta^{k (x+1)} = \zeta^k \zeta^{k x} = \zeta^k \chi_k(x)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:rep_independence}.
    \item $(\Delta \chi_k)(x) = (E - I)\chi_k(x) = (\zeta^k - 1)\chi_k(x)$, and $(\nabla \chi_k)(x) = (I - E^{-1})\chi_k(x) = (1 - \zeta^{-k})\chi_k(x)$.
    \item Composing the two differences:
    \begin{equation}
    \Delta(\nabla \chi_k) = (\zeta^k - 1)(1 - \zeta^{-k})\chi_k = (\zeta^k - 1 - 1 + \zeta^{-k})\chi_k = (\zeta^k + \zeta^{-k} - 2)\chi_k.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Exact Lattice Dispersion in $\mathbb{C}$]
Under the canonical root $\zeta = e^{2\pi i / L}$, the Laplacian eigenvalue evaluates to:
\begin{equation}
\zeta^k + \zeta^{-k} - 2 = -4 \sin^2\left(\frac{\pi k}{L}\right).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Using Euler's formula with angle $\theta = \frac{2\pi k}{L}$:
\begin{equation}
\zeta^k + \zeta^{-k} = e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta} = 2 \cos\theta.
\end{equation}
Using the trigonometric identity $\cos\theta = 1 - 2 \sin^2(\theta/2)$:
\begin{equation}
\zeta^k + \zeta^{-k} - 2 = 2 \cos\left(\frac{2\pi k}{L}\right) - 2 = 2 \left( 1 - 2 \sin^2\left(\frac{\pi k}{L}\right) \right) - 2 = -4 \sin^2\left(\frac{\pi k}{L}\right).
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Formal Correctness vs.\ Physical Implications: Trigonometric Truncation vs.\ Continuum Dispersion]
In continuum physics, the spatial kinetic operator is $-\partial_x^2$, whose Fourier transform yields the quadratic dispersion $p_{\mathrm{phys}}^2 = (2\pi k / L)^2$. In the long-wavelength continuum limit where the mode index is small compared to the lattice size ($|k| \ll L$), we approximate $\sin(\pi k / L) \approx \frac{\pi k}{L}$, and the exact lattice dispersion becomes:
\begin{equation}
-4 \sin^2\left(\frac{\pi k}{L}\right) \approx -4 \left(\frac{\pi k}{L}\right)^2 = -\left(\frac{2\pi k}{L}\right)^2 = -p_{\mathrm{phys}}^2,
\end{equation}
recovering the continuous kinetic Laplacian $-\partial_x^2$. 

On the discrete lattice, the trigonometric dispersion $-4 \sin^2(\pi k / L)$ is bounded between $-4$ and $0$ for all modes in the Brillouin zone. High-momentum modes near the Brillouin zone edge do not diverge quadratically; their energy saturates at $-4$. This natural trigonometric bending provides an intrinsic, exact ultraviolet regularization that eliminates divergences without needing arbitrary momentum cutoffs.
\end{remark}

\section{Chapter Summary and Literature Comparison}

In this chapter, we developed the finite Fourier transform bridging spatial and momentum representations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The algebraic character layer is defined over any commutative field $K$ containing a primitive $L$-th root $\zeta$, requiring only $L \cdot 1_K \neq 0$.
    \item Dual character orthogonality yields two-sided algebraic inversion $T \circ S = S \circ T = L I$.
    \item In the complex Hilbert setting ($K = \mathbb{C}$), scaling by $1/\sqrt{L}$ yields the unitary Fourier isometry $U$, satisfying the Parseval/Plancherel identity.
    \item The discrete difference operators and Laplacian $\Delta\nabla$ are diagonalized by characters, yielding the exact bounded lattice dispersion $-4\sin^2(\pi k / L)$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Comparison with Standard Continuum Literature.}
In standard condensed matter treatments of 1D systems, such as Giamarchi~\cite{giamarchi2004}, Gogolin et al.~\cite{gogolin1998}, and von Delft and Schoeller~\cite{vondelft1998}, the Fourier transform is formulated with continuous plane waves $e^{i k x}$, integrating over a continuous wire $[0, \mathcal{L}]$. Field commutators are delta functions $[\psi(x), \psi^\dagger(y)] = \delta(x-y)$, which are singular distributions requiring smearing with test functions.

In this finite-lattice framework, all transforms are finite sums over the discrete group $\Lambda$. Kronecker deltas $\delta_{x, y}$ replace Dirac delta distributions, characters $\chi(k, x)$ are finite algebraic roots of unity, and the Fourier transform is a strictly unitary operator on finite-dimensional spaces. This ensures that when we construct spatial fermionic operators in Chapter~\ref{ch:fermions}, the CAR relations are preserved exactly with zero distribution-theoretic artifacts.
